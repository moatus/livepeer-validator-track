# M1.2 Review: Short Listening Guide

About twelve minutes. Built only from the validator-track workbench and repository, as at commit eec258d. No identifiers are spoken. A short reference list sits at the end.

**Status.** M1.2 is reviewed.

---

## One. The question

M1.1 mapped what each mechanism does and separated what anyone can compute from what rests on outside facts or judgment. M1.2 goes back over the same twelve mechanisms and asks a sharper question. Where does each mechanism's success depend on something not yet established?

Where the answer is yes, the step writes a critical question. A critical question starts as proposed. It is not a finding that the mechanism fails. It is a statement of what would have to hold, what would change the assessment, and what follows if it resolves badly. M2 is where such questions get tested.

The step produced nine critical questions, four conclusions, and four new findings. Every one of the twelve mechanisms ends up touching at least one critical question.

---

## Two. The method: eight lenses in a fixed order

Each mechanism is read through eight lenses. The order runs from what can be computed, to what can be seen, to who decides, to how people respond.

One, calculation: what is reproducible once rules are fixed. Two, observation: what information would settle the question, who holds it, what it shows as opposed to merely records, whether it could be faked or withheld, and what it costs. Three, inference: what a record establishes, and what it does not. Four, policy and authority: who decides, and whether the decider has a stake in the outcome. Five, individual incentives: what each participant is paid for. Six, collective behavior: what a group holding many seats or addresses could do. Seven, social and operational effects: how errors and disagreement land on people. Eight, system feedback: what flows back into other mechanisms.

Alongside the lenses, the step keeps the separate claims apart. Payment, execution, correctness, service quality, availability, independent demand and economic value are different claims. The reward formula uses payment, stake share and fee share directly, and the dishonesty finding through the median. It has no direct input for independent demand or economic value. The step asks which of these distinctions the reward objective actually needs.

The pass started with three mechanisms that follow paid work: the eligible reward, the honesty score, and the claim. Then it covered the other nine.

---

## Three. What the lens pass found on the score and the claim

Two mechanisms carry most of the weight.

On the score: the median is reproducible once the list of validator scores is fixed. But the list is not fixed. There are no rules for a missing score, a validator choosing not to score, or an even count. More deeply, a score records a judgment. It does not establish that the judgment is warranted. And no procedure is specified for the affected operator to see the reasons for a score or to answer it.

On the claim: the payout is the eligible reward times the median current at the claim, applied once. The paper does not name who submits the claim. The delay provides a wait. It does not establish that the median at the end is a warranted finding about that round. The summary says seven days and the parameter table says seven rounds, and the step does not treat those as one interval. An ordinary late claim and a claim timed to a higher median look the same at payment. And whether a pending reward counts toward bonded stake before the score applies is an open accounting question.

One caution the step repeats: choosing to wait is not itself dishonesty.

## Four. The nine critical questions

They fall into three clusters.

### Cluster one. Can the judging work?

**First: does the evidence warrant an adverse finding?** Can the evidence a validator has separate self-dealing, fee fabrication and incorrect results from their legitimate lookalikes, well enough to justify cutting a reward? Three pairs are named. Related-party production, or an operator's own testing, versus reward-seeking self-funding. A legitimately variable output versus an incorrect result. A genuine fee surge versus a fabricated spike.

If the separation fails for a pair, a cut can fall on legitimate activity, or a named dishonesty can be paid. A median does not repair weak evidence. The concern weakens if a defined observation set separates each pair at a stated error and cost inside the delay. A narrow standard that cuts only a specified violation would be enough. Proof of independent demand for every job is not what the paper requires. The proposed test: name the alleged violation first, list what a validator could know by the end of the delay, and state acceptable error and cost before looking at outcomes.

**Second: will validators produce defensible assessments?** Validators are operators, review takes effort, and others can respond. The risk named is drift: toward uniform full scores, or toward coordinated low scores. The step also notes that the overlap between operator and validator is the paper's own reason for the role. The one percent validator share is not the whole benefit of the seat.

**Third: can an operator protect its reward without disproportionate effort or influence?** The paper's protection against an unfair score is being a validator, which gives standing to push back. Only top-staked nodes can register. The paper specifies scores and a median, not a hearing or an appeal. If defending a reward needs cost or relationships, open entry can coexist with a practical barrier to earning. The concern weakens if otherwise similar established and new operators get comparable access to reasons and comparable response effort.

### Cluster two. Does the reward pay for the right thing?

**Fourth: does more rewarded fee require the intended contribution?** This is the self-funded real usage question from M1.1, now stated as a critical question. Payment, execution and correctness checks can all pass while a party that controls the purchase raises the fee input. The paper assigns the remaining defense to validator scoring and the delay.

The concern weakens, for a named emissions concept and stake position, if every added self-funded real job has a nonpositive return once the network diversion, resource cost, retained shares and claim timing are counted. It strengthens only if the missing contribution has been defined and the return is positive under named assumptions. The step adds that a positive figure would not be a result for the litepaper as a whole. And useful internal production and release testing are legitimate. The paper does not require an independent customer for every fee.

**Fifth: can the LPT price behind the mint and the buyback resist being moved?** For a fixed dollar mint target, a lower price produces more tokens. For a fixed buyback budget, a higher execution price buys fewer tokens to burn. A party that profits by distorting either price changes issuance or burn. The paper itself asks how to secure a price source that is not subject to manipulation. Choosing the third emissions concept removes price from the mint input, but not from the buyback.

### Cluster three. Is the structure sound?

**Sixth: can delegators move stake to useful operators in time?** If stake does not reach productive, under-staked nodes in time, their rewards stay capped below their fee shares. If stake follows reported returns regardless of the work behind them, delegation does not direct reward toward useful work.

**Seventh: does an open listing give a new operator practical access to paid work?** Gateways may prefer operators with stake, and the paper says that can be left outside the protocol. If so, stake would shape both terms of the reward formula. The paper's path gives a new node work first and delegation afterward. Stake-weighted selection would make the work depend on the stake it is meant to attract.

**Eighth: can governance change the rules without deciding particular nodes' rewards?** Governance can change parameters and replace the scoring implementation. The paper states no limits on replacements and no transition rules. The concern is a vote that settles one node's pending reward outside general criteria. A general change that lowers rewards is not itself the concern.

**Ninth: is a majority of validator seats costly to control?** The paper relies on this cost to secure the median. The step notes that seats are counted in nodes, not in stake. One interest could spread stake across several nodes. Declined seats also move down to lower-staked nodes. The question asks what the stake defense costs, not whether anyone would attack.

## Five. The four conclusions

**Four dependencies sit outside the reward arithmetic.** Evidence for validators' judgments, customers' selection of nodes, market prices, and control behind addresses. The fee split, shares, mint division, median and counters can all be reproduced. These four cannot be supplied by writing the arithmetic. Identifying them does not show they cannot be met.

**Stake does three jobs.** It sets a node's reward ceiling, ranks nodes for validator seats, and carries weight in governance votes. Which balances count as votes is open. So where stake sits bears on delegation, access to work, and the cost of seats. High stake, running several nodes and common ownership are not misconduct.

**Self-funded real work can pass payment, execution and correctness checks.** The reward formula sees a fee share and a stake share, not who paid. The network half of the fee is not recovered. Depending on the emissions concept, added fees can raise, hold or lower the dollar mint. The paper's own words are that validator scoring and the seven-round delay are what handle a node taking a larger share of a fixed pool. But the delay is argued for a one-round spike, and correctly executed self-funded jobs need not produce one. Whether it pays remains a hypothesis.

**Validators hold positions in the rewards they score.** They are competing operators. No way to see reasons or to answer a score is specified. The step is careful here: this describes the rules, not how anyone behaves. A supported low score of a competitor is the role working as intended.

---

## Six. Four new findings, all inferences

The highest reward ceilings sit with the nodes eligible to validate. A gateway preference for stake would reach the fee term as well as the stake term. Validator seats are counted in nodes, not stake. And at the second emissions concept's cap, more fees mean a smaller dollar mint, while a node's share can still rise.

---

## Seven. What M1.2 does not claim

That any mechanism fails. A critical question is proposed, not proven. That anyone would attack, collude or defraud. That common ownership, several nodes, high stake or waiting to claim is misconduct. That the paper must change. Missing procedures are things to specify. And none of the nine has been tested. That is M2.

---

## Eight. For the reviewer

Is each question framed fairly to the paper? Each has a stated way to weaken it. Does that path look realistic, and not rigged?

Are nine the right questions? One number is unused, and the list could be missing a question or carry one too many.

Do any consequence statements read as verdicts, not conditionals?

Is the score-and-claim emphasis earned, or does it reflect the project's starting concerns more than the paper's text?

Do the four dependencies outside the arithmetic ring true to anyone who runs part of the network?

Do the claim-timing observations match how claiming would work in practice?

---

## Nine. One paragraph

M1.2 re-read all twelve mechanisms through eight lenses and found nine places where success depends on something not yet established: whether evidence can separate abuse from legitimate lookalikes, whether validators will follow the evidence, whether an operator can answer a score, whether more fee means more of the intended contribution, whether prices resist manipulation, whether delegation moves in time, whether a newcomer wins work, whether governance stays general, and whether the validator set is costly to take. It named four dependencies outside the reward arithmetic, kept the self-funding risk a hypothesis, and tested nothing. That is deliberately left for M2.
