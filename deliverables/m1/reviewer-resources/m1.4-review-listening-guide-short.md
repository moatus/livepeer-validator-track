# M1.4 Review: Short Listening Guide

About twelve minutes. Built only from the validator-track workbench and repository, as at commit eec258d. No identifiers are spoken. A short reference list sits at the end.

**Status.** M1.4 is reviewed.

---

## One. The question

M1.1 to M1.3 worked from the paper. M1.4 turns to what exists today. For each of the twelve mechanisms, which current contracts, node software and participant roles can be reused, which must change, what is new, and what is unknown?

This is a map from today's system to the paper's. It is not a plan to build, and not a verdict on the current system. It is also a source reading only. Nobody checked what is actually deployed.

---

## Two. How the map was made

Three source trees were read. The protocol contracts. The node software. And the clearinghouse, which is the hosted service that signs payments and bills customers.

For each contract, off-chain component or participant role that matters to a mechanism, the step wrote a component record. Thirty-seven were recorded: fourteen contracts, fourteen off-chain components, and nine roles. Each records the source version inspected and the mechanisms it serves.

Each component carries a change label saying what the paper would do to it. Nineteen are modify. Seven are possible design options. Five are retain. Five are unknown. One is remove. Where the paper names no implementation, the label says whether it is an analytical judgment, a design option, or unknown because the source does not settle it. A label is a mapping, not a decision.

Each mechanism then received exactly one of three outcomes. A counterpart, with its source. None found, within a stated scope. Or unresolved, with the remaining question.

The tally: ten mechanisms have a counterpart, though only partial, and for one of those it is supporting only. Two have none found: honesty scoring and buyback and burn.

---

## Three. What stays

Several current features match what the paper keeps.

Any address can already publish a service address in the service registry, with no bond check. Delegators already bond, unbond and rebond, and operators set reward cuts and the share of fees delegators receive. The paper keeps these mechanics. LPT remains the stake and issuance token. Treasury voting weighted by stake, with a timelock, is already present, and the paper refers to existing treasury governance for the treasury's share of emissions. Rounds still bound the accounting. And one selector in the gateway code already mixes stake, price and a random weight, after performance and price filters.

---

## Four. What changes the most

Six contrasts stand out.

**Registration.** Today the node software's activation path requires a positive self-bond, and fee and reward eligibility use a stake-ranked active set with a maximum size. The paper separates an uncapped registry with no bond from a voluntary validator set. No region field was found in today's advertisement.

**Payments.** Today's payments are probabilistic tickets backed by ETH, priced in the smallest unit of ETH. The paper prices in dollars, settles in USDC, and splits each fee between node and network. No USDC settlement and no node-network split was found. The paper does not say the ticket must remain. The clearinghouse records payments as customer usage in dollars, but sets billable and network amounts equal and does not settle on chain. A ticket also shows a payment, not that the work was done.

**Reward.** Today an active operator's reward is the round's mintable tokens times its share of total active stake. The treasury cut comes off first, then the operator's cut divides the rest with delegators. No fee-share minimum and no honesty input was found. The paper caps the reward at the smaller of fee share and stake share, then scales it by the median score.

**Claiming.** Today's claim function ignores its end-round argument, claims through the current round, and adds the LPT reward to bonded stake. There is no seven-round hold and no claim-time score. This also bears on an open question: whether an unsettled reward counts as stake before the score applies.

**Stake movement.** Today the unbonding length is a parameter. And the call that transfers a bond processes the rebond immediately. So the paper's twenty-one rounds, including transfers, is not what this code does. The paper itself leaves open whether transfers are removed or delayed.

**Emissions.** Today inflation moves with the ratio of active stake to total supply. The paper lists that rule, with annual caps, as one of three open alternatives. Fee-linked issuance and a dollar cap were not found.

---

## Five. What is new

Four things were not found in any of the three sources.

A zero-to-one node score, or a median of such scores. An opt-in validator set, or any way to pay validators. A purchase of LPT with fees, because the existing burn entry points do not buy LPT with fees. And a score-contract address on the controller.

For scoring and for buyback, the step records none found. It lists the nearby features that look related but are other mechanisms: ticket validation, local performance scores, classifier scores, treasury ballots, and a legacy verifier slashing path. The source comments on that path say the verifier is unset, which was not checked on the chain.

The step is careful with these absence claims. They cover the three sources named. They do not cover other repositories, and they do not cover deployed behavior.

---

## Six. What remains unresolved

Deployed behavior was not checked. So no component records a deployed version. Still open are the active-set size, the unbonding period, who owns the contracts, and whether the source matches deployed bytecode.

The paper describes today's cap and a seven-round unbonding period. These sources do not confirm either one, since both are parameters. The clearinghouse pins a node-software image that is not the commit inspected. Whether the clearinghouse is deployed at all is unresolved.

The step treats these as unresolved, not as absent. Design questions already open from M1.1 also remain open: which emissions alternative, payment security once the operator cap is gone, which fee quantity and window enter a reward, whether every way of moving stake waits, and which authority changes parameters as distinct from treasury voting.

---

## Seven. Two new design questions

M1.4 found two gaps that were not yet design questions.

**Pending rewards before the claim-time score.** Before the score applies, does any part of a round's reward increase bonded stake, later stake share, voting power or a commission snapshot? Today's claim adds the reward to bonded stake immediately, so the paper's claim-time score would need a rule here.

**Voting power without a required self-bond.** Today a registered operator's votes assume a self-bond, and the supply used for voting is active stake. Once a node need not self-bond and the operator set is uncapped, which balances count as voting power, and what is the quorum denominator?

---

## Eight. What M1.4 does not claim

That the current system is deficient. Differences from the paper are what the paper would change, not faults. That a missing feature cannot be built. That anything was verified on chain or in production. That absence in these three sources means absence everywhere. And that a change label is a decision to modify, retain or replace. The labels map; they do not choose.

One other thing is worth knowing. The step produced no new numbered conclusions. It received two from M1.1, on supporting work and on accounting inputs. Its own findings are the four points on the step page, summarized above, plus the component and design question records.

---

## Nine. For the reviewer

Does the tally look right: ten partial counterparts and two none-found? Is anything labeled none found that a reader knows exists?

Are the change labels reasonable? Nineteen modify and five unknown say a lot about how far the paper sits from today's system.

Does the transferBond finding match what someone who runs the contracts knows?

Do the six contrasts match how an operator or delegator experiences the system today?

Are the stated limits of the search clear enough? In particular, nothing was checked on chain.

If someone can read deployed contract state, they can settle the open items directly: active-set size, unbonding period, owners, bytecode, and clearinghouse deployment. The step invites exactly that as a contributor note.

---

## Ten. One paragraph

M1.4 read the protocol contracts, the node software and the clearinghouse, and recorded thirty-seven components against the twelve mechanisms. Ten mechanisms have a partial counterpart today. Honesty scoring and buyback have none in the sources searched. The biggest differences are in registration, payment, reward calculation, claiming, stake movement and emissions. Four things are entirely new: a score and its median, an opt-in validator set, a fee-funded buyback, and a score-contract address. It added two design questions, left deployed behavior explicitly unchecked, and passed everything on to M2.1.
