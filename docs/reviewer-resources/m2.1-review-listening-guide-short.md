# M2.1 Review: Short Listening Guide

> Historical September-source review guide. This preserves the original pilot narrative and then-current status commentary. For current October assumptions and the next work, use the [M2 specification](../m2/SPEC.md) and [Slice 2 preparation](../m2/SLICE-02.md). This guide is not an October review or acceptance.

About thirteen minutes. Built only from the validator-track workbench and repository, as at commit 31edc45. No identifiers are spoken. A short reference list sits at the end.

**Status.** M2.1 is in progress. Its first slice is reconstructed. The remaining slices are not.

---

## One. The question

M1 took the litepaper apart. It named twelve mechanisms, asked what each depends on, picked the cases that matter, and mapped what exists today.

M2.1 puts the mechanisms back together. Its question is this. When the mechanisms are assembled, and every rule the paper leaves open is filled in with a stated interpretation, do the choices and exposures that participants face produce the behavior the paper intends?

The shift is from calculation to behavior. M1 looked at one mechanism at a time. M2.1 follows a participant, or several acting together, across mechanisms and over time. It asks who can do what, who knows what, when, and what each choice does to the next.

One thing to hold onto. M2.1 runs no tests. It sets up the tests. It fixes the rules, the roles, the timing and the accounting, so that the next step, M2.2, can run small examples that mean something.

---

## Two. How the work is cut up

M2 is worked in slices. A slice takes one concern and carries it through all three steps: reconstruct, test, consolidate.

The first slice is a pilot. It takes the concern M1 ranked as central: self-funded real usage. That is a participant paying its own node for real, correctly executed jobs, in the hope that the inflation reward exceeds the cost.

So M2.1, as it stands, is the reconstruction for that one concern only. Validator incentives, delegation, access for new operators, rule changes and the token price each wait for later slices.

The method has four moves. Write down the relationships the paper intends and what each needs. Fill each open rule with a labeled reading, comparing variants only where they could change the answer. Follow the cases, cooperative first, then market pressure, then strategic. Then rate each relationship: supported, conditional, contradicted or unresolved.

Two guardrails. Never quietly pick the reading that makes a concern look strongest. And a deviation that pays while everyone else is held still is not an equilibrium, and says nothing about how common it is.

---

## Three. What M1 handed over, and the order

M1 handed on six questions in a proposed order. M2.1 is meant to confirm or change that order. For the pilot, it keeps the two linked questions first.

First, does the concern survive accurate evidence and competent review? Second, when does extra self-funded activity pay, and where does that stop? Third, which of the paper's defenses work, and at what cost? Fourth, who bears the burden of defending a reward? Fifth, does an open listing give a new operator paid work? Sixth, could one realistic response by anyone change the result?

Why judgment first? The paper says a larger share of a fixed pool is what validator scoring and the seven-round delay are for. So the paper's other defenses pass their leftover cases to scoring. If scoring doesn't work, the other answers change. An early rank means the outcome could move more of the final recommendation. It does not mean the concern is more likely to hold.

---

## Four. The cast and the choices

Three situations are set side by side.

An outside customer buys a job from a listed node. This is the paper's ordinary path, the cooperative reference.

The operator runs real jobs on its own node to test a release or its billing path. This is a legitimate lookalike. The paper does not exclude it.

The operator pays its own node for extra real jobs, aimed at the reward. Records are accurate, execution is real, and reviewers are competent. The case asks whether any concern remains after every check succeeds.

Notice what is not assumed. Common ownership is not misconduct. Profit-seeking is not misconduct. Nothing here is faked.

Serving outside demand, testing, adding jobs, declining them, claiming at the first chance, claiming later, and waiting are all distinct choices. Doing nothing is one.

---

## Five. What the reward is meant to buy

This is the center of the step. The paper's principle for the bootstrapping phase is fees for honest work.

There are two ways to read that. One reading accepts any correctly executed, useful work, including internal work, alongside outside work. The other asks whether fees deliberately manufactured, with no productive use, supply the contribution the paper wants.

The paper settles neither. It does not require independent customers. So the step refuses to merge three answers that are easy to blur. Is it profitable? Is something the reward is meant to buy missing? And can a validator defensibly say the operator was dishonest? Each is its own question, and a result on one does not answer the others.

---

## Six. The two limits, and the gap between them

The paper offers two different limits, and M2.1 keeps them apart. The stake minimum limits a node's share of the reward: the smaller of its fee share and its stake share. The emissions concept sets how big the pool is.

If a cap binds, the pool stops growing. But a larger share of a fixed pool can still be taken. The paper's answer is scoring and the delay.

The paper argues for the delay using a one-round fee spike, which gives real time to investigate and drive the score to zero. But correctly executed self-funded jobs need not produce a spike. So the question handed forward is whether seven rounds yield evidence anyone can act on for real work. Time passing is not evidence arriving.

---

## Seven. The rule register

The heart of the reconstruction is a register of twenty-two rules. Each row names a place where the paper is silent or ambiguous, gives one base reading, carries variants, and says whether the choice changes the result.

Variants are of three kinds: calculated in the next step, stated and carried without calculation, or deferred. You don't need all twenty-two. Here is what they mean in groups.

**What counts as a fee, and when.** Settled gross payments count in the round they happen. No refunds. Stake and fee windows line up. Annual figures become rounds using an illustrative three hundred sixty-five, since no source fixes a round's length. The paper says days in places and rounds in its table, and the step does not reconcile them.

**How the reward is computed.** The base case is the main text: smaller of fee share and stake share, scaled by the median once. The appendix describes it differently: proportional to work, with no stake term. Both are carried, because they differ on one thing, whether a stake ceiling exists at all. The register records that this choice can flip the sign of an example. Illustrative only, in the fixed-pool control: a node with five percent of stake and ten percent of fees adds one percent more fees. Under the main text it loses. Under the appendix it gains. That shows what the rule choice does, not a finding about anyone.

**How big the pool is.** Three emissions concepts from the paper's unfinished draft, plus a fixed-pool control. The control isolates share-taking; it is not a proposed policy. Only year zero of the first concept is used. Later years and the lower twenty-eight point eight ratio are deferred.

**Where leftovers go.** Unassigned or withheld reward is not redistributed, and does not go to rivals or accusers.

**The organization's own accounting.** Fee split fifty-fifty, with the network half leaving the organization and being burned. The organization keeps all its node's reward, and its validator share is held at zero. Execution cost is an illustrative thirty cents per dollar of fees. Payments to itself cancel. Testing carries a positive use value; reward-farming carries none.

**Claiming and scores.** Claim at the seventh round by default. A later claim changes the payout only if the median changed. No claim leaves the reward uncollected, with no invented deadline or forfeiture. The median is applied once, at the claim. A variant lets it recover later, so a claimant could wait. The chance of a zero median is a sensitivity, not a detection estimate.

**What counts as self-dealing.** Two standards are carried. A narrow one: conduct specified in advance as deceptive, such as misrepresenting who paid or why. A broad one: funds circling back to a commonly controlled node, funded for that purpose, with no productive or testing use. Neither bans common ownership.

**When a lower score is warranted.** Two readings, since the paper states neither. Reading one needs evidence of the violation, so an unexplained job stays unknown. Reading two lets validators lower the score if the operator doesn't explain within the delay, which puts the burden on the operator. The risk of reading one is missed violations. The risk of reading two is false cuts on legitimate work like release testing.

## Eight. Who controls what

A control map covers three setups.

First, an independent customer. They own the money, choose the node, and know their own use. They own no stake and no seat.

Second, an integrated organization. It controls payer, routing, node and stake, and knows the purpose and cost. Validators can question the jobs. The organization can answer with payment records, or voluntarily disclose a release plan. Nobody can read motive from a fee total.

Third, the same organization with outside delegators. Mapped, not modeled. Delegators raise the node's ceiling but not the organization's control of purchases, and their receipts would be outside income.

One restraint. The map names who could ask and who could disclose. It imposes no hearing, no duty to defend and no appeal right. The paper specifies none, and M2.1 does not supply one.

---

## Nine. The timeline

One timeline puts it all in order.

Start of round n: stake share and cut terms are snapshotted. In round n: the job, the settlement and the fee measurement. From n through n plus seven: evidence can arrive, and validators can investigate. At n plus seven: the earliest claim, with the then-current median applied once. After that: a later claim, or none.

Stake movement runs on its own separate clock: twenty-one proposed rounds, including bond transfers. Stake that someone moves at the moment of the job is still locked past the first claim. Fresh capital is not barred. Principal is not confiscated. A penalty can affect rewards while capital waits.

---

## Ten. Five intended relationships, all conditional

The paper expects five links. M2.1 rates each one.

Useful paid fees open a reward opportunity. Conditional: settled fees must track work of the intended kind, and fee share must be below stake share.

The stake minimum limits self-dealing by low-stake nodes. Conditional: it holds only while fee share meets the ceiling and stake does not rise to cover it. A party can raise the ceiling with more stake, its own or delegated or fresh. Moving existing stake is slowed. Whether that reopens the route, at what cost, is not tested here.

The direct fee cut rewards work above the ceiling. Conditional: a self-funded job's cut only partly recovers an internal payment. It is not outside income.

Fees can enlarge the first concept's mint. Conditional: only while the cap isn't binding.

A binding cap stops pool growth but not share-taking. Conditional on the unused room under the ceiling, or on the appendix reading.

None is promoted to a finding. Conditional is the honest rating before tests.

One structural observation. The nodes with the highest reward ceilings are the same nodes eligible to validate, so a validator can be both beneficiary and scorer. High stake is not misconduct, and this describes the rule, not any operator. Whether it changes the reasons to score carefully goes to the validator slices.

## Eleven. Tests confirmed or narrowed

The proposed tests from M1 were checked against the reconstruction, and narrowed to stay small.

The economic test begins with the outside customer, then adds the operator's testing, then the self-funded jobs. It uses both reward readings, all three emissions concepts plus the fixed control, starts above and below the stake ceiling, and the response sensitivity. Average and marginal returns, sign changes and false cuts are reported separately.

The evidence test compares only the self-funded case beside release testing, under both standards, with evidence revealed in the order it could actually reach validators before the first claim. Tolerances for missed violations and false cuts are stated before outcomes are looked at. The related-party pair moves to a later slice. A synthetic packet can test logic. It cannot measure detection accuracy or reviewer effort, and the step says so.

---

## Twelve. The conclusion

One conclusion was written, held at the level of inference. The pilot now has stated readings for fee attribution, node entitlement, emissions, ownership, costs, prices, claim timing and self-dealing. Control and timing are mapped for three setups. Both reward readings are carried separately.

The rule for reading the results was set in advance. A positive return strengthens the concern only if a missing contribution has been defined. A nonpositive return, or a reading that accepts the activity as honest work, narrows it. Neither sign establishes misconduct, how common it is, or what validators can detect.

---

## Thirteen. What M2.1 does not claim

It does not claim self-payment is profitable, or that it isn't. It does not claim validators can or cannot tell the cases apart. No figure is a forecast or a parameter. It chooses no emissions rule, no reward reading and no self-dealing standard. It says nothing about how common any behavior is. And the whole system is not yet reconstructed. One slice is.

---

## Fourteen. For the reviewer

Is a slice the right unit to approve? The step is marked in progress and covers one concern. Are you approving the first slice, or the whole step? The milestone overview and README still describe M2.1 as handoff received and nothing run, which is out of date.

Do the three setups cover the right cast?

Are the base readings fair? Would a different reading have been equally natural? The two reward readings are the clearest case.

Do the two self-dealing standards and the two score-warranted readings span the real disagreement, or is a third position missing?

Does any sentence slide from a gap into a fault, or a conditional into a conclusion?

And one open practitioner question: how fast does stake really move? The timeline depends on it, and it is unanswered.

---

## Fifteen. One paragraph

M2.1 reassembles the litepaper's mechanisms into one system and, for the pilot concern, self-funded real usage, fixes the rules, roles, timing and accounting needed to test it. It carries two readings wherever the paper is silent, separates profitability, missing contribution and defensible dishonesty, rates the paper's five expected links as conditional, and narrows the next tests to stay small. It runs nothing and claims no result. It is one slice of a larger reconstruction.
