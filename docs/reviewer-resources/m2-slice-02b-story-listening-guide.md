# Three stories about real self-funded work

*Drafted research. This guide explains a bounded October litepaper comparison. Owner research review remains pending. The spoken account ends before the source notes.*

The starting point is useful service. An outside customer pays for a job, the operator performs it, and the network divides the payment between the service side and token buyback and burn. Useful internal work belongs beside that ordinary sale. An operator might fund release testing or production work within its own organization. Common ownership does not make that dishonest.

Ordinary competition comes next. Changing prices, attracting outside customers or receiving different routes changes the fees on which rewards depend. Then the comparison asks a narrower strategic question: what happens if an organization deliberately pays its own operator for real jobs solely to increase its rewards, with no useful internal benefit credited?

That purpose is a declared premise known to the modeler. Validators are not given a way to read it. Payment, execution, quality, availability, independent demand and economic value remain different claims. The paper still needs to define any reward distinction it requires beyond honest real work.

The main-text reward rule takes the smaller of a node’s fee share and its stake share. Below that stake limit, extra fees can increase reward entitlement. At the limit, more fees cannot increase the reward at fixed stake. Fees can still be earned through ordinary sales.

Three anonymous roles make the differences concrete. They occupy the largest, middle and lower stake positions in a measured network snapshot. The snapshot supplies fees, stake and cuts. The model adds assumed jobs, execution costs and capacity, keeps stake fixed and gives full honesty scores. Recorded dollar fees are replayed as hypothetical USDC. Address-owned stake is used as a proxy for organizational ownership, which it does not prove.

There is another distinction to keep in mind throughout. Every added purchase costs cash. A gain here means additional unsold LPT, valued at the stated token price, exceeds that cash loss. It does not mean the organization has sold tokens or earned cash profit.

## The largest role

The opportunity begins with headroom. This role has much more stake share than fee share, so additional paid work can increase its reward. But most of the reward belongs to external recipients. With the native cuts and ownership proxy, only about four percent of its gross node reward stays within the organization. The large stake position therefore does not translate directly into a large retained gain.

Its one-round spending choice invoices about seventy-eight dollars. After the fee recovery and execution expense, it loses about eighty-two dollars in cash. That is the model’s cash-loss allowance. It is an analytical convention, not a known balance sheet or a protocol limit. The model also does not establish how the organization finances the invoice before recovering fees.

What it retains is about ninety-three additional tokens. At the stated valuation, those tokens leave about seventy-seven dollars of net value after the cash loss. The gross reward increase is far larger, because external reward recipients receive most of it.

Who loses? Honest pools with positive initial rewards that are limited by fee share lose about nine percent individually. Pools protected by their stake caps remain protected until dilution makes their fee shares fall below those caps. Across all other pools together, the reward loss is about six percent. External delegators bear most of that token loss. Their outside customer payments do not change in this comparison.

The actor’s extra reward does not all come from peers. Almost nine tenths of its gross gain comes from allowance that would previously have stayed unminted. The remaining tenth corresponds to other pools’ lost rewards. This fraction describes gross claimability, before organizational retention. It is not the organization’s share of the gain. Actual issuance rises even though the scheduled ceiling stays fixed, and burn remains a separate supply effect.

Why stop? The cash-loss convention binds before the stake cap. Physical job capacity does not bind. More funding can permit a larger profitable action in this case. Once the stake cap is reached, however, further purchases consume cash without adding reward.

What reverses the result? Holding these native shares and retentions fixed, the arithmetic says the opportunity closes when valued scheduled node allowance becomes small enough relative to fees. Aligning fees with stake also removes the headroom. Higher execution costs or lower collectible token value can remove the retained gain. Those are conditional boundaries, not forecasts about this source participant.

## The middle role

The opportunity is different at the start. This native role already earns a fee share far above its stake share. It has exhausted its inflationary reward headroom under the main-text rule.

Its spending choice is therefore zero. There are no additional controlled invoices and no added cash loss at the optimum.

Its retained gain is also zero. More self-funded fees cannot raise its reward at fixed stake. This leaves ordinary outside sales available, with their direct fee receipts.

Who loses? Nobody loses reward from a zero additional purchase. The model includes diagnostic purchases that could dilute other pools, but this actor would lose cash without gaining reward from them. Harmful spending and profitable spending are separate questions.

Why stop? Here the stake cap is an effective immediate defense. It works before the cash convention or physical capacity matters.

What reverses the result? Giving this same rank artificially less initial business creates headroom. In standardized controls that assume the organization retains everything, those below-cap cases become positive. They are upper bounds, not estimates of what the native participant would do. The bounded standardized appendix comparisons also remove the stake cap and change the middle role’s choice. That difference requires an authoritative entitlement rule; the study does not choose one. Buying or attracting more stake remains untested, including its cost, availability and timing.

## The lower role

The opportunity starts from no recorded fees and a very small stake share. Additional real jobs can unlock a small allowance. This role’s native reward retention is high, so most of that small gross reward stays inside the modeled organization.

Its spending choice is tiny: about a third of a dollar in added invoices, with roughly a quarter-dollar cash loss.

Its retained gain is about ten additional tokens, leaving about seventeen dollars of valued gain under the stated assumptions.

Who loses? Fee-limited peers lose at most about four hundredths of one percent at this stop. Collective loss is smaller still, roughly one and a half tokens across the other pools, with most borne by external delegators. Outside fees remain fixed. Where a pool had no initial reward, its percentage loss is undefined rather than zero.

Why stop? The small stake cap binds well before either funding or physical capacity. Giving this role more cash cannot enlarge profitable reward-taking at fixed stake. It can finance additional harm-only spending, which loses money after the cap.

What reverses the result? Enough outside business to fill its cap removes the headroom. A sufficiently low allowance-to-fee ratio, lower token valuation or higher expense can close the positive region. Its small reach follows from its stake share and initial business, not from the rank label alone.

## Following the conditions

The broader comparisons move along four selected scales: today’s measured allowance-to-fee ratio, a smaller mid-path ratio, near parity and a fee-leaning control. These compare the valued scheduled node allowance with fees, not actual issuance. They are stress controls, not adopted targets or a projected development path.

All standardized below-cap core cases are positive under full retention and the chosen costs and valuation. Many stop at stake caps; a few stop at the cash convention; some near-parity cases stop before the cap because the next purchase no longer pays. All aligned controls choose zero. The three computed fee-leaning confirmations also choose zero. Separate low-valuation controls remove the opportunity in all their selected cases.

Distribution matters, but changing a stake structure also reallocates fees in these fixtures. The comparison cannot attribute the whole difference to concentration. Initial business matters directly: less headroom means less opportunity and less profit-seeking dilution. A single fixed-stake actor’s harm is bounded by that headroom, and individual loss is different from collective loss.

The network charge provides another real limit. Even a controlled invoice priced much higher cannot recover the network’s half under full fee retention. Higher price can reduce execution expense per invoiced dollar while leaving cost per physical job unchanged. It does not remove that charge floor. This price comparison holds the original physical capacity and cash allowance fixed. It provides no market-price detector and no new evidence of dishonest behavior.

Capability labels by themselves change no accounting. More outside demand or a stipulated competitor response can change denominators, costs and retained gain. These are imposed comparisons, not evidence of how customers or operators would actually respond.

The timing comparison uses one cash stock across four earning rounds. No tokens are sold, and cash is neither replenished nor reinvested. These timelines use standardized full retention rather than the native largest role’s small retained share. Only the constant and equal-total ramp schedules, with matched actual spending and costs, isolate pacing. The constant schedule earns slightly more than the matched ramp; both outperform putting the same stock into one round under these conditions. Diminishing returns to added fee share explain why spreading can help. The escalating schedule asks for more spending before the stock truncates it. The middle and lower roles have caps below the stock, so their timelines support no pacing conclusion. None of these schedules is a multi-round optimum.

Claims remain pending until their collection dates. Extending the delay moves collection later without funding another job or changing final full-score value. Delay could matter through evidence or scores, but elapsed time alone supplies neither. Existing evidence judgments depend on explicitly disclosed control and funding, and authentic, adequate, safe useful-work material. Hidden purpose, hidden ownership and the newly modeled volumes receive no new verdict.

What remains is specific. The reward formula and claim accounting need authoritative implementation detail. Any required contribution distinction needs policy definition and obtainable evidence. Practical funding, stake movement, discovery, costs, valuation and defensible scoring need their retained studies. The paper’s strengths remain visible: permissionless sales, separate fee receipts, a fee-independent ceiling, fixed-stake caps, cash leakage and delayed collection. The results identify conditional concerns and limits; they do not establish observed abuse, exit, prevalence or equilibrium, and they select no new mechanism.
