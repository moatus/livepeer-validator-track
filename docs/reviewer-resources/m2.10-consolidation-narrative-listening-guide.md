# M2.10: The close pass, and what M2 hands to M3

*Drafted research narrative. Owner review of M2.10 remains pending. About ten minutes. No identifiers are spoken; a few numbers appear where they carry the meaning. The material lives on the local branch `m2-close-consolidation`, not on main.*

## Why this piece exists

By the end of M2.9 you had built up a lot. Self-funded work, capability pricing, strategic self-funding, the evidence study, operator response, validator scoring, and the current-system measurements. What you did not have was a statement, for every critical question the program set out to answer, of where it stands. M2.10 is that statement. It is the close pass.

It has two jobs. First, finish four studies that had been deliberately deferred: delegation in time, access to paid work, rule changes while rewards are pending, and the token price question. Second, take all nine critical questions and give each one an honest disposition, using only evidence the program already has. Then hand M3 a list of unresolved inputs, with the paper's retained strengths standing right beside them.

Notice what it does not do. No new chain collection, no practitioner contact, no new simulation, no successor design. It is a finite pass, and it says so.

## The one new computation

Only one thing is genuinely new, and it is small. It answers a single question. Can stake reach a productive, understaked node in time, and can the same fee opportunity be manufactured by controlled purchases?

The setup reuses the measured network snapshot and the same accounting engine from the earlier strategic self-funding study. A mid-ranked operator, ranked fiftieth, is lifted to a two percent share of stake and fees. Then the same amount of work arrives in two flavors. In one, it is productive work from outside customers. In the other, the operator buys the work itself. The jobs are identical. The fees are identical. Only the payer differs.

Then four movement lanes are run. No movement. Fresh capital arriving from outside. Existing stake that unbonds and rebonds. And existing stake that uses the delayed transfer the paper allows.

The first result is the one that connects to the paper's lock. The timing rule mechanically gives fresh capital about twenty-two eligible rounds of the elevated fee opportunity, while existing stake that must wait gets about one. So the destination operator's delegators collect roughly forty-four thousand tokens in the fresh lane, versus about two thousand in the existing-stake lane. The lock really does slow movement of existing stake. But fresh capital does not face that same wait. That is the tension: the paper wants fee and stake information to guide delegation, and the lock makes that guidance arrive late, for some capital and not others.

The second result is who pays for moving. A delegator who unbonds and rebonds ends up about eight thousand tokens worse off than one who stayed put, because they forgo source receipts while waiting. Delayed transfer comes out slightly ahead in tokens, about fifteen hundred, though a few dollars behind in fees. So the paper's two options for handling transfer are not equivalent. They carry different costs to honest participants.

The third result repeats a theme from earlier slices. The productive case and the controlled-purchase case give the same token rewards. The operator's cash differs: about plus four hundred dollars in one, minus thirteen hundred in the other. Rewards alone cannot tell you which world you are in. This is a reading of incentives under a research premise, not a detector.

Two guardrails to keep in mind. Fresh capital's outside return is unknown, so you cannot say whether anyone would move. And the current-system measurements from M2.7 show only about three and a half percent of stake moving in ninety days under today's rules, which is a window average, not a response time and not a forecast.

## Three source readings

The other three deferred studies are readings, not experiments.

Access to paid work. The paper promises uncapped listing and uncapped ordinary fees. It does not specify how a buyer's traffic is allocated. So this reading traces how today's gateway code actually selects operators: it requires self-bonded admission to a limited pool, filters, then mixes stake, price, and random weights, with defaults weighted heavily to stake. Sessions get reused. The result is conditional. If traffic is rerouted to a cheaper new operator, the economics follow, and the earlier slices showed that. Whether a new operator actually wins traffic is not established, and these are defaults, not what production gateways run. Listing is a strength. Practical access remains unmeasured.

Rule changes while rewards are pending. Three timelines, no numbers. A general parameter change. A general scoring replacement. A hypothetical exception aimed at one operator. The reading separates ordinary evidence-based median updates, which the paper already provides, from changing the rules underneath a reward already earned. What it finds is a gap, not a flaw: the paper does not specify who may change what, with what notice, applying to which pending rewards. Neither guaranteed immutability nor inevitable abuse follows. It is a specification hole, handed to the later procedure work.

The token price. Here the history matters. The September draft depended on a mint-price oracle. The October paper removes that, by using a token-denominated ceiling. That dependency is retired. But the buyback itself, the route, the pool, the cadence, the slippage, remains open, and the paper's own appendix asks for DEX research. The earlier valuation controls, where all nine selected cases chose zero action even with the token at a tenth of the price, are valuation tests, not liquidity or manipulation-cost measurements. So this question is routed intact to the buyback stage of M4, with a concrete list of what it needs: a chosen design, block-pinned depth, and a bounded adversary payoff.

## The nine dispositions

Then comes the consolidation. And here is the candid headline: all nine come out unresolved. But not identically unresolved, and that is what makes it useful. Each says what kind of unresolved it is: a specification gap, missing evidence, an incentive question, or a policy decision.

Evidence for an adverse finding. Across every pair of cases, purpose does not separate. The one exception is geometry. If the terms are authenticated, the work is linked to a node, the bytes are held by an independent custodian, and the record is accessible safely and in time, a breach of the agreed output size does separate from legitimate variation. Remove any condition and that goes away.

Validator incentives. Cooperative scoring works. But identical pay across actions does not buy effort. There is also a subtle but important result: under the main-text reading, a lower score does not move reward to rivals, because it is never minted. Be careful with this one. The paper explicitly states non-minting for allowance that is unavailable under the smaller-of-fee-and-stake rule. It does not state it for score-withheld rewards. That second part is a completion the study inherited and the paper must specify. The appendix describes a different allocation that could change the result.

Real self-funded work. Under the October fifteenth research premise, paying yourself for jobs only to collect rewards counts as dishonest, so the positive gains in the models identify an incentive concern where they occur. But honest useful related-party work is distinct, and the contribution policy is genuinely undecided. The study is clear on one limit: it cannot measure acquisition cost, and it found nothing that reopens the earlier decision to stop that inquiry. That stop is a research judgment awaiting your review.

Delegation, reward-allegation response, access, rule changes, seats, and price each get the same treatment. The seat result carries the concrete number from before: seventeen new seats out of thirty-three needs about six and a half million tokens, roughly a fifth of stake. That is a size, not a price, and addresses are not organizations.

## What M3 gets, and what it keeps

M3 receives six unresolved inputs: the contribution objective and the evidence it requires; alignment of compensated judgments with independent participation; delegation under the lock; fair response and access; practical market entry; and bounded rule authority over pending claims. The buyback goes to the first M4 stage, the practical evidence-access question to the second, and detailed dispute procedure to the third.

Next to those, the handoff keeps the paper's strengths: uncapped listing and ordinary fees, the fixed-stake smaller-of rule, the fee-independent decaying ceiling, non-minting of unavailable allowance, shared investigation, evidence-updatable medians, and the stake and lock barriers. "No expansion needed" is stated as a valid M3 outcome.

One late addition, from October twenty-first, is the most strategic paragraph in the document. M2 surfaced things the paper does not address at all. In the measured round, the scheduled allowance, valued at the declared price, is about fifty-seven times the outside fees, and seventy-three of one hundred pools earn nothing. So the paper's machinery governs how a large reward pool is divided across a thin fee layer, not how that layer grows. Five gaps sit inside it: nothing pays for service quality, because the score targets dishonesty; customer routing and discovery are unspecified; capacity ahead of demand earns nothing, so supply for a new capability waits on fees that need that supply to exist; a capability has no organizational unit or owner; and buyers have no stated remedy when a job fails. These are candidates for M3 to evaluate. Naming them is not an expansion.

## My candid read

What works: the discipline. The prospective contract was frozen before outcomes, an independent reviewer audited the whole M2 chain against actual sources and found real reporting defects, and they were fixed without rerunning experiments. The distinction between paper-stated and inherited non-minting is exactly the kind of catch that keeps this honest.

What to be aware of: this is mostly synthesis. One small computation plus readings. Nine of nine unresolved is defensible, but it also means M2 mainly narrows and labels the questions rather than answering them. Several important needs, recognition and movement evidence, realized access, and beneficial control, are explicitly unassigned. The thin-fee-layer paragraph is the closest thing to a finding that points forward, and it is a single measured round, so treat it as a lead.
