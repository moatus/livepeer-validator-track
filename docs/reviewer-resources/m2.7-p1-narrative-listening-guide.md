# M2.7 (P1): What the current-system data notes mean

*Drafted research narrative. Owner research review remains pending. This guide is about meaning, not data points. The spoken account ends before the reference list.*

About eight minutes. No identifiers are spoken. A few numbers appear where they carry the meaning.

## Why this piece exists

Slice 2b ended on an open lever. Everything it measured held an operator's stake fixed. It said the stake cap is only as strong as the cost of moving stake, and that cost was unknown. A related question sat beside it: how concentrated is stake, and what would it take to control the validator seats?

This piece does not model any of that. It measures today's system. It reads about ninety days of real activity on the live network, ending in mid-October, and it reads the code that decides which operators get work. Think of it as taking readings from the real machine, so the earlier arithmetic can be calibrated against something.

One rule governs everything it says. These are readings of today's system under today's rules. They are not a forecast for the litepaper. Today the unbonding wait is seven rounds, redelegation is instant, and the reward does not depend on the smaller of fee and stake share. The paper proposes a twenty-one round lock and a different reward rule. Behavior under today's incentives does not tell us how people behave under the paper's.

## How it was done

The method is careful, not clever. It pulled every stake-changing event and every redeemed ticket from the window, from two independent sources, and matched them one to one. All of the more than twenty-two thousand events reconciled exactly, with none missing. Then it built tables offline, with an independent verifier checking hand-worked examples.

There is no model and no simulation. The value is that the readings can be trusted and repeated.

## What it found

There are four readings.

### One. Stake moves, but not much, and movement is not a price

Over the ninety days, about three and a half percent of active stake changed operators through instant redelegation. Transfers of stake ownership that crossed operators were about a quarter of one percent. At least half of matched withdrawals came at the earliest allowed time, the seven-round minimum. Long waits that cross the window edge are not counted.

So today stake is easy to move, and it mostly stays put. There is little reason to move it. Under the paper's reward rule, an operator's stake would set its ceiling, and that would give people a reason to move stake. So this rate is a starting calibration, not a prediction. A low rate today does not show stake will stay put when moving it pays. It also does not show that attracting stake is cheap or expensive. Event counts measure activity, not anyone's decision speed.

For the earlier work, this puts a real-world number beside the fixed-stake assumption. It does not weaken the concern or settle it.

### Two. Stake is concentrated, and the shape is stable

A few operators hold a lot. Three or four hold a third of all stake. Eight hold half. Eighteen or nineteen hold two thirds. That shape barely changed across the window.

### Three. A seat threshold is a size, not a price

The paper elects validators from the top-staked operators. Under stated premises, the study asks how much new stake would seat a majority. Those premises are thirty-three seats, a majority of seventeen, no existing coordination, and incumbents holding still. Under them, it takes about a fifth of all staked tokens, placed across seventeen new nodes. If the biggest holders have already coordinated, or some top seats decline, the number falls. In one stated case, the stake of just the two largest pools would already be enough, if one party controlled and could move it.

This is the closest the study comes to your question about whales. It says how large a controlling stake would be. It does not say what it would cost to acquire, whether the stake is for sale, or who controls it. Addresses are not organizations. The study states this on every row: a stake threshold, not an acquisition cost.

Because of that, nothing reopens. The earlier stake-cap work would reopen only if acquiring stake were shown to be cheap and fast. This data shows neither. It leaves the question exactly as unknown as before, but now with the structure around it measured.

### Four. A fee spike has a shape, and the code has a default

The window contains a real settlement spike. The biggest round settled about nineteen times the typical round, made of only eight redemptions, dominated by one recipient and one sender. That is a concrete shape for the kind of one-round spike the paper's delay was designed around. But settlement is not jobs, and it is not independent demand. The study draws no conclusion about any spike. It supplies a reference for the evidence work that comes next.

The gateway code reading adds a related caution. By default, picking an operator for new work weights stake heavily, adds a measurable random share, and gives price no weight beyond a cap filter. Known sessions are reused. A new operator also has to register, self-bond, enter a limited pool and pass several checks. These are source defaults, not what gateways run in production. The earlier slices stipulated that a discounted offer might win rerouted traffic. This reading neither confirms nor refutes that. It shows the answer depends on how gateways are configured.

## How this fits the story

The thread from slice one has been the same: the worry's size depends on conditions, and several conditions are unobserved. Slice 2b named stake mobility as the biggest of those, along with ownership and token value. This piece measures what it can about stake and finds structure, not a verdict.

The honest summary is that nothing here closes a critical question. Stake is concentrated, moves modestly under today's rules, and has a computable size for seat control. Whether control can be bought remains unknown. The settlement series and the selection code are inputs, not answers.

## Where it leads

The delegation-in-time example runs later regardless, now with measured movement beside it. The evidence study receives the spike reference, and it opens next. The scoring study receives the seat thresholds for its control-cost table. The access question receives the selection reading beside the rerouting controls. None of those is decided here.

## What this does not say

It does not give an acquisition cost, a forecast, or a statement about the paper's rules. It does not show anyone is acquiring stake, coordinating, or gaming a spike. It covers one ninety-day window. And it treats address activity as address activity, not organizations.

## The story in one paragraph

Earlier work left stake mobility as the open lever. This piece measured the real system and found that stake is concentrated, stable, and moved modestly under today's rules, with a computable size for seat control and a default selection rule that leans on stake. None of that is a price, a forecast or a verdict. It narrows what is unknown without changing which part is unknown: what control costs, and how people would behave when the paper's incentives apply.
