# M1.3 Review: Short Listening Guide

About twelve minutes. Built only from the validator-track workbench and repository, as at commit eec258d. No identifiers are spoken. A short reference list sits at the end.

**Status.** M1.3 is reviewed.

---

## One. The question

M1.2 produced nine critical questions: places where a mechanism's success depends on something not yet established. M1.3 asks which concrete cases test those questions, and which of those cases matter most.

A case is a specific situation. Who acts, what each party can see, and what outcome would support, narrow or reject the concern. Where possible, a case is paired with its legitimate lookalike: ordinary activity that can look the same.

The step ends with a ranked list of cases covering all nine critical questions. Every case is hypothetical. None has been run. Running them is M2's work.

---

## Two. How cases are built

Each critical question gets cases in a fixed order. First a cooperative case, where the mechanism works as the paper intends. Then ordinary market pressure. Then selected strategic behavior. Suspicious activity always sits beside its lookalike.

Each case records the same four things. The situation. What each party can observe, including what they cannot. What is ambiguous, and exactly what would support, narrow, or reject the concern for that case. And what the paper itself says should happen, with the line cited.

The set now holds thirty-nine cases. Sixteen came from M1.1. Twenty-three were created in M1.3. By type: ten cooperative, six market-pressure, eleven adversarial, and twelve lookalikes. The lookalikes outnumber the adversarial cases, which is deliberate.

One principle is repeated throughout. Profit-seeking is not misconduct. Common ownership is not misconduct. And the results the paper intends carry the same weight as the worrying ones: a full score on ordinary work, a low score that tracks a defined violation, stake moving to a productive node, a general rule change under a stated transition.

---

## Three. How the cases are ranked

The ranking asks one thing. Could the outcome of this comparison change a later recommendation?

An early rank means the outcome could change more of what follows. It does not mean the concern is more likely to hold. That distinction is stated explicitly.

The judgment comparisons come first, for a structural reason. The paper hands the remaining defense to validator scoring and the seven-round delay. It rests the median's defense on the stake cost of taking a majority of seats. So whether evidence separates the pairs, whether validators score from it, and what a majority costs, all condition the later questions about fees, delegation, access, governance and price.

---

## Four. The ranked pairs, in plain words

There are thirteen ranked entries. Here are the ones that carry the most.

**First: real related-party work beside self-funded farming, with the operator's own testing.** An affiliate under common ownership buys real jobs for its own production. Versus a party paying its own node for real, correct jobs in the hope the reward exceeds the cost. Versus an operator paying its own node to test a release. All three leave the same kinds of payment and execution record. A validator cannot see motive in a fee total, and the paper gives no related-party test. If both cases supply the contribution the reward is for, different treatment may be unnecessary.

**Second: a one-round fee spike beside a genuine surge.** The spike is the attack the paper names, and the delay is its answer. Outside customers buying a burst of real jobs leaves the same fee total. The concern holds if nothing a validator can obtain by the end of the delay differs between them. It narrows if some observation separates them.

**Third: a legitimately variable output beside an incorrect result.** AI media output can legitimately differ between runs. The paper has no promised-terms test and no standard of acceptable variation. A check against promised terms can be possible even when an identical replay is not.

**Fourth: a supported low score of a competitor beside coordinated validators zeroing a rival.** Validators compete with the nodes they score. A low score backed by published evidence is the role working as intended. The same low score with nothing behind it is the failure. Related cases cover full scores despite contrary evidence, the ordinary cost of careful review, and holding back while evidence is incomplete. No recusal rule and no appeal are specified.

**Fifth: seats.** Top-ranked operators decline for ordinary reasons, and seats pass down to independent operators. Beside one interest placing a majority of seats through several nodes. Beside an operator who runs several nodes for ordinary reasons. Seats count nodes, and stake records do not show common control, so the three can look alike. A cheap majority is a condition for capture, not evidence anyone would use it.

**Sixth: answering a score.** The same evidence question answered by an operator with access to reviewers and by one without. This tests whether defending a reward depends on the evidence or on standing.

The remaining entries cover outside purchases and API resale versus a thin upstream margin; stake moving to a productive node versus stake following fees the operator paid; a new listing winning work versus selection weighted by stake; an on-time claim, an honest late claim, and a claim timed to a higher median; a general rule change versus one that reaches a single pending reward; an ordinary price move versus a deliberate one; and thin coverage showing up as lower fees with the score staying full.

One case is kept but unranked: whether an ordinary node price could count as dishonest. The paper names no such criterion, so a test would have to invent one.

## Five. The three conclusions

**Seven ranked comparisons turn on who paid, who controls, which rule, or why.** In seven comparisons, the concerning case and its lookalike leave the same kinds of record. They differ in who paid, who controls the addresses, which rule was applied, or why someone acted. Telling them apart needs an observation beyond those records, or a finding that the distinction is not needed for the question. The cases are unrun. They show what the records contain, not that no other evidence can separate the pairs. The proposed test compares public records with one additional source whose disclosure is explicit.

**The judgment comparisons rank first.** Because the paper's other defenses pass their remaining cases to scoring, and the median rests on seat cost. The fee, delegation and access comparisons build on those. The governance comparison asks whether a rule change can override their outcomes. The price comparison isolates a separate effect on amounts.

**Five more critical questions need an open rule stated before their cases can run.** Selection, bond transfers, seat rules and missing scores, voting power and transitions, and the buyback price rule. Each result will hold only for the rule stated. The cases say which rule to state. They do not choose it. Where two plausible rules give different results, the case shows how far the concern depends on the choice.

---

## Six. What M1.3 does not claim

That any case describes current behavior. That any concern holds. That a paired case is a strong or weak test before it is run. That the paper must change. And that earlier means likelier: rank reflects how much a result could change later recommendations.

---

## Seven. For the reviewer

Are the lookalikes genuinely fair? A concerning case beside a legitimate one only helps if the legitimate one is plausible.

Is the ranking test the right one? Ranking by what could change a recommendation, not by what seems most worrying, is a choice worth confirming.

Is anything missing? Thirty-nine cases for nine questions is a sample, not a census.

Do the observable parts ring true for people who run nodes, validate, or delegate? The nine practitioner questions from M1.1 are where this can be checked.

Is the unranked case reasonably left out?

Does the cooperative case in each pair carry real weight, or is it a formality?

---

## Eight. One paragraph

M1.3 turned nine critical questions into thirty-nine concrete cases, mostly paired so that suspicious activity sits beside its legitimate lookalike. It ranked them by whether their outcome could change a later recommendation, putting the judgment comparisons first because the paper leans on scoring and seat cost to defend the rest. It found that in seven comparisons the pair leaves the same kind of record, so separating them needs something beyond those records or a finding that no separation is needed. And it noted that five more questions cannot be run until an open rule is stated. None of the cases has been run. That is M2.
