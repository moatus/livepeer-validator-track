# M2.1 to M2.3, Slice One: Listening Guide

> Historical September-source review guide. This preserves the original pilot narrative and then-current status commentary. For current October assumptions and the next work, use the [M2 specification](../m2/SPEC.md) and [Slice 2 preparation](../m2/SLICE-02.md). This guide is not an October review or acceptance.

About thirty minutes, in three parts of roughly ten to twelve minutes each, one for each step. Built only from the validator-track workbench and repository, as at commit 31edc45. No identifiers are spoken. A short reference list sits at the end.

**Status.** All three steps are marked in progress. This guide covers the first slice only: self-funded real usage.

## One. What this covers, and how to listen

M2 is worked in slices. A slice takes one concern and carries it through three steps. M2.1 reconstructs how the mechanisms behave together. M2.2 runs small tests. M2.3 sorts what the tests mean and decides what goes to M3.

The first slice is a pilot. It takes the concern M1 ranked as central: a participant paying its own node for real, correctly executed jobs, in the hope that the inflation reward exceeds the cost.

Part A is the reconstruction. Part B is the two tests. Part C is the consolidation and the handoff. Then comes what the slice does not claim, and questions for you as reviewer.

A note on numbers. Every number in this guide is illustrative arithmetic under stated assumptions, unless it is marked as the paper's own proposal. None is a forecast, a measurement, or an approved parameter.

## Two. The slice in one breath

The question has two halves, reported separately on purpose.

First, a money question. Under each way the paper might be completed, does one extra self-funded real job return more to the organization that controls both payer and node than it costs? And where does that answer change sign?

Second, an evidence question. By the time the reward can first be claimed, can a validator tell that job apart from the operator's own useful release testing, using evidence it can actually obtain?

Why this concern first? In M1 it was ranked as the central untested risk. If fees drive reward, a party might pay itself for real work and collect more reward than the job cost. Proving the job happened does not settle it, because the job really did happen. It is also small enough to exercise every stage of the M2 process once, which is the second purpose of a pilot: to learn how the process behaves before the larger slices run.

The short answer, before the detail. The money question has a mixed answer: the paper's defenses work in some regions and fail in others, and which region you are in depends on rules the paper has not chosen. The evidence question has a plain answer for the one pair tested: the evidence does not separate them. And one thing sits underneath both and is still open: what the reward is meant to buy.

## Part A. M2.1: Reconstruct

## Three. Why reassemble

M1 took the litepaper apart into twelve mechanisms. M2.1 puts them back together and follows a participant across mechanisms and over time. Who controls what, who knows what, what happens when, and what each choice does to the next.

M2.1 runs no tests. It fixes the rules, roles, timing and accounting, so that the tests mean something. Its discipline is to fill every unsettled rule with a labeled reading, carry two readings where it is unsure, and never quietly choose the one that makes the concern look worst.

M1 handed over six questions in a proposed order, and the order has a reason. Questions about judgment come first: does the evidence separate the cases, will validators score from it, what does a majority of seats cost. The paper's other defenses pass their leftover cases to scoring, so if scoring does not hold, the answers about fees, delegation and access all change. An early rank means an outcome could move more of the final recommendation. It does not mean the concern is more likely to be true.

Each intended relationship will be rated supported, conditional, contradicted or unresolved, under stated conditions. And one caution is built in. A deviation that pays while everyone else is held still is not an equilibrium, and says nothing about how common it is.

## Four. The cast

Three situations are set side by side.

An outside customer buys a job from a listed node. This is the cooperative reference.

The operator runs real jobs on its own node to test a release. This is the legitimate lookalike. The paper does not exclude it.

The operator pays its own node for extra real jobs, aimed at the reward. Records are accurate. Execution is real. Reviewers are competent. The case asks whether any concern remains after every check passes.

Common ownership is not misconduct. Profit-seeking is not misconduct. Nothing here is faked.

## Five. The question underneath: what the reward is meant to buy

The paper's principle for the bootstrapping phase is fees for honest work. There are two ways to read that.

One reading accepts any correctly executed, useful work, including internal work, alongside outside work. The other asks whether fees deliberately manufactured, with no productive use, supply the contribution the paper wants.

The paper settles neither, and does not require independent customers. So the slice keeps three answers apart. Is it profitable? Is the intended contribution missing? Can a validator defensibly call the operator dishonest? A result on one does not answer the others. Hold on to this. It returns at the end.

## Six. The two limits, and the gap

The paper has two different limits. The stake minimum limits a node's share of the reward, which is the smaller of its fee share and its stake share. The emissions concept sets the size of the pool.

If a cap on the pool binds, the pool stops growing. But a larger share of a fixed pool can still be taken. The paper's own answer to that is "validator scoring and the seven-round delay".

And the delay is argued using a one-round fee spike, which gives time to investigate and drive the score to zero. Correctly executed self-funded jobs need not look like a spike. So the open point is whether seven rounds produce evidence anyone can act on.

## Seven. The rule register

The core of M2.1 is a register of twenty-two rules. Each is a place where the paper is silent or ambiguous. Each gets a base reading, carried variants, and a note on whether the choice changes the result. The groups that matter:

Reward formula. The base case is the main text: the smaller of fee share and stake share, scaled by the median once. The appendix describes it differently, as proportional to work, with no stake term. Both are carried, because they differ on one thing: whether a stake ceiling exists at all.

Pool size. Three emissions concepts from the paper's unfinished draft, plus a fixed-pool control. The first ties the mint to fees through a ratio, below a cap. The second subtracts any fee increase from a starting mint. The third adjusts by participation. The fixed-pool control is an analytical device that isolates share-taking. It is not a proposed policy.

Leftovers. Unassigned or withheld reward is not redistributed, and does not go to rivals or accusers.

The organization's accounting. Fee split fifty-fifty as the paper proposes. The network half leaves the organization and is burned. The organization keeps all of its node's reward. Execution cost is an illustrative thirty cents per dollar of fees. Payments to itself cancel. Testing carries a use value; reward-farming carries none.

Claiming and scores. At the seventh round by default. Later or never are choices, with no invented deadline and no assumed forfeiture. A later claim pays a different amount only if the median has changed. The base case keeps the median at the claim fixed. A variant lets it recover later, so a claimant could wait for a better score. Whether a claimant can see scores in advance is not stated by the paper.

Prices and timing. The mint's dollar target is converted to tokens at a price, and the reward is valued at a claim price. Both are held equal and positive. Any effect of the buyback on the price is held at zero, stated as an assumption. Annual figures become per-round figures using an illustrative three hundred sixty-five rounds a year, because no source fixes how long a round is. The paper says days in some places and rounds in its parameter table, and the step does not reconcile them.

Stake and pending rewards. Stake share is a snapshot at the start of the round and is held fixed during the addition. A pending reward does not count as stake before it is claimed. The paper does not say whether it does. That matters because it decides whether the reward could compound.

Validators. The organization's own validator receipt is held at zero. A node at twenty percent stake would plausibly sit in the top thirty-three and be eligible for a seat, so a real receipt could exist. It is left out of the payoff and carried forward.

Self-dealing. Two standards. Narrow: conduct specified in advance as deceptive, such as misrepresenting who paid or why. Broad: funds circling back to a commonly controlled node, funded for that purpose, with no productive or testing use. Neither bans common ownership.

When a lower score is warranted. Two readings, since the paper states neither. Reading A needs evidence of the violation, so an unexplained job stays unknown. Reading B lets validators lower the score if the operator does not explain the jobs within the delay, which puts the burden on the operator. The risk of A is missed violations. The risk of B is false cuts on legitimate work.

The register has one more habit worth naming. For each row it asks whether the choice changes the result. Several do. The reward formula, the emissions concept, the cost of the job, and the chance of a zero median can each flip a sign. Others were stated but not computed. That is how the later tests stay small and honest about what they skipped.

## Eight. Control and timing

A control map covers three setups, asking who controls the money, the routing, the node addresses, the stake, the claim and the recipients.

The independent customer owns the money and chooses the node. They know their own use. They own no stake and no seat, and they receive the output, not any operator reward.

The integrated organization controls payer, routing, node and stake. It alone knows the purpose, the cost and what the outputs are used for. Validators and shared investigators can question the jobs. The organization can answer with payment records, or voluntarily disclose a release plan. Nobody can read motive from a fee total.

The third setup adds outside delegators. It is mapped but not modeled. Delegators control their capital and can move it. They raise the node's ceiling without gaining control of its purchases, and their receipts would be outside income.

One restraint. The map names who could ask and who could disclose. It imposes no hearing, no duty to defend and no appeal right. The paper specifies none, and M2.1 does not supply one.

One timeline. Round n: the job, settlement and fee measurement. Through the seventh round after: evidence may arrive. At the seventh: the earliest claim, with the current median applied once. Stake movement runs on its own twenty-one-round clock.

Stake movement is separate. If someone asks to move stake at the time of the job, the twenty-one rounds run well past the first claim. Principal is not confiscated, and fresh capital is not barred.

Five relationships the paper expects were each rated conditional, not tested.

Paid fees open reward opportunity: only if settled fees track work of the intended kind and fee share is below stake share.

The stake minimum limits low-stake self-dealing: only while fee share meets the ceiling and stake does not rise to cover it. A party can raise the ceiling with more stake, its own, delegated or fresh. Moving existing stake is slowed. Whether that reopens the route, at what cost, was not tested.

The direct fee cut rewards work above the ceiling: a self-funded job's cut only partly recovers its own payment. It is not outside income.

Fees can grow the first concept's mint: only while its cap does not bind.

A binding cap stops growth but not share-taking: share can still be taken from unused room under the ceiling, or under the appendix reading.

One structural observation. The nodes with the highest reward ceilings are the same nodes eligible to validate, so a validator can be both beneficiary and scorer. That describes the rule, not any operator.

The tests were then narrowed to stay small: the money test covers the outside customer, then testing, then self-funded jobs; the evidence test covers only the self-funded case beside release testing.

## Part B. M2.2: Test

## Nine. The first test: where does an extra job pay?

The first test is a calculation. It takes one added real job and asks whether it returns more than it costs, under both reward readings and all emissions concepts, from starting positions below and above the stake ceiling. Anyone can rerun it, and a separate checker uses independently entered constants.

There are four setups in the calculation. An outside customer's job, as the baseline. The operator's useful testing. The self-funded job. And declining the job, which has no increment at all. The self-funded and testing jobs differ only in whether the output has use value. A related-party job looks the same on paper as testing, and is handled with it.

The paper's three emissions concepts matter here because they answer a different question: how much does the pool grow when fees grow. In the first, the mint is a multiple of fees, thirty-two times in year zero, until a fifty million dollar annual cap binds. In the second, the mint starts at a fixed amount and any fee increase is subtracted, so more fees shrink the pool. In the third, the mint follows participation and ignores fees. The fixed-pool control holds the pool constant, to isolate who takes the share. The third concept calibrated to the control gives identical signs, so it adds nothing under fixed participation.

Controls: the organization owns all its stake and keeps all its reward. Prices are equal. Execution costs thirty cents per dollar. The median is full. Others' choices are held fixed. One round only.

The key number is the unrecovered cost. For each dollar of added fees, the organization loses the network half, fifty cents, plus thirty cents of execution. So eighty cents per dollar must be earned back by extra reward. The step also separates average return, over the whole addition, from marginal return, on the next dollar. They can disagree, and the report keeps both.

## Ten. Where the stake minimum works

Take a node whose fee share is already at or above its stake share, in a fixed or shrinking pool, under the main-text reading. Extra fees add no share. The organization just pays eighty cents per dollar. Every added job loses. In the second concept it is slightly worse, because the pool itself shrinks as fees rise.

Take a node below its ceiling. Here extra fees do raise its share, so adding jobs first pays, then stops paying. At the illustrative stake shares of one, five and twenty percent, the sign changes at added fees of roughly a fifth, about equal to, and nearly four times the existing outside fees.

So the stake minimum works as intended where it binds. It is not a cap on fees, and not a defense when there is room under the ceiling.

Put it as a story. Imagine two operators, each adding the same self-funded job. The first already earns more fee share than its stake share. It pays the network half, pays its costs, and gets nothing extra back. It loses. The second has a lot of stake and few fees. Its extra fees do lift its share, and for a while the reward gained exceeds the eighty cents it pays. Past a certain size the share hits its ceiling and the gain stops while the cost goes on. The lesson is not that the defense works or fails. It works for one operator and not the other, and the difference is where each sits relative to its ceiling.

## Eleven. Where it does not

Three places.

First, uncapped pool growth. In the first concept below its cap, extra fees enlarge the pool. A node already at its ceiling gains reward amount from that growth without gaining share. Return per dollar works out as ninety-four percent, times the ratio of thirty-two, times stake share, minus eighty cents. That is positive once stake share is above about two point seven percent. At five and twenty percent it stays positive until added fees reach roughly four-fifths of, and three times, existing outside fees. The cap then binds, at about forty-two percent of existing fees in this setup. After that the marginal job loses eighty cents a dollar, though accumulated return can stay positive.

Second, the appendix reading. With no stake ceiling, the fee-work reading gives positive returns where the main text gives losses. In one illustrative example, a one percent addition earns a large gain per dollar under the appendix and loses eighty cents under the main text. Break-even points exist, but only at additions of roughly ten to fifty times existing outside fees. The step is careful: that is an algebraic boundary, not a practical size defense.

Third, a corner where honest work loses. In the second concept with the appendix reading, a shrinking pool can make even outside work and release testing lose over a band of sizes.

The first concept's year-zero ratio is the paper's own. The slice also notes that at the paper's year-four ratio of eight, the break-even stake share rises from about three percent to about eleven percent, so the tested five percent flips from positive to negative. That is why a follow-up is recommended.

## Twelve. What a score cut would do

A median of zero removes the node's whole round reward, including reward from honest outside fees. So a cut can reverse a positive return.

The step expresses this as a threshold, not a prediction. In one illustrative position, the self-funded job flips to a loss if the chance of a zero median exceeds about half of one percent. The release-testing lookalike, with its use value, flips at about one percent. If a false cut lands on useful testing, the organization loses the same reward.

The number is a payoff sensitivity. It is not an estimate of how likely validators are to cut. The evidence test says why.

## Thirteen. The second test: can evidence separate them?

The second test is a synthetic evidence comparison. The rules were set before outcomes were looked at. The alleged violation is named first. Acceptable error and effort are stated first, and committed in their own earlier commit, before the packet and verdicts.

The false-cut tolerance is strict. A cut on legitimate testing, or on the unmodified self-funded case merely for self-payment, is unacceptable. Missing proof is accepted as a limitation, not counted as a success. The narrow standard needs a labeled variant, because the unmodified case has no deceptive act. The packet is read in the order evidence could reach validators. Public records are compared with public records plus one disclosed source: the operator's release plan and the change that followed, arriving five rounds after the job.

Six claims are judged separately: payment, execution, correctness, independent demand, economic value, dishonesty.

## Fourteen. What the evidence shows

Payment, execution and correctness are established. Independent demand is contradicted, given known common control, without making independent customers a requirement. Economic value stays unknown. Dishonesty is unknown for the self-funded case.

Every verdict, at every level, under both standards and both readings, is the same: does not separate.

The disclosed plan clears the testing case. It does not prove anything against the self-funded case. Disclosure is one-sided.

Reading A, evidence required, avoids unsupported cuts, but leaves the allegation unresolved. Reading B, explanation burden, gives a different picture. With public records only, a cut for silence can reach legitimate testing and fails the false-cut tolerance. With timely, authentic, safe disclosure, testing is spared and the unexplained job may be cut. But that cut answers silence, not evidence. It would equally fall on any legitimate job whose explanation can't be shared safely or in time.

Effort was also bounded in advance. Routine review is one pass over the public records. A contested review adds one focused question, one operator response, and one disclosed source checked against the jobs, and all of it must fit before the first claim. The disclosed branch fits those ceilings by construction. Whether it would in practice is not measured.

So the readings differ in who is exposed, not in what the evidence supports. Honest engineers bear a preparation burden under B. And the packet is synthetic: authenticity, access and timing are assumptions. The checker verifies structure only. It cannot show detection accuracy or reviewer effort. Being unable to infer motive from one invented file does not show it is impossible for every job.

## Fifteen. What M2.2 leaves out

The other pairs for the evidence question wait for the next slice. The tests on validator incentives, delegation, protecting a reward, access, rule changes, seat cost and the price have not run. Outside delegation, added stake, higher costs, later ratios, unequal prices and later median recovery are deferred.

## Part C. M2.3: Consolidate

## Sixteen. What this step does

M2.3 sorts everything by what would resolve it. A specification, which is build work and says nothing against the paper. Better evidence. A change in incentives. Or a policy choice. Items that plausible completions of the paper leave unresolved, and areas the paper leaves unaddressed, go to M3.

## Seventeen. What would resolve each design question

Across the workbench there are thirty-two design questions, one of them retired into others, and each carries a classification of what would resolve it. Twenty-six involve a specification. Sixteen involve better evidence. Fourteen involve an analysis of incentives. Eleven involve a policy choice. They overlap, since one question can need several. Only six are resolved by a specification alone.

That last number matters. A question that a specification alone resolves is build work and says nothing against the paper. The other twenty-six need evidence, incentive analysis or a decision by someone with authority.

For this slice, the build list holds fee and stake snapshots and denominators, reconciling the two reward descriptions, where leftover reward goes, the conversion of dollar targets into tokens, cuts, who may claim, finality, and the treatment of pending rewards. These are necessary implementation work. Choosing an emissions concept, choosing the fee split, and defining the intended contribution are policy choices. Access to evidence and whether a response is feasible are evidence work. Validator pay, careful scoring, and the overlap between high ceilings and seats are incentive work.

## Eighteen. The defenses, one by one

Each defense is assessed by its operating condition and what it costs honest participants.

The network fee and burn works when unrecovered fees and costs exceed the extra reward. It defeats the loss regions, not the positive ones. Honest outside work also pays it.

The stake ceiling works on share at fixed stake. Uncapped pool growth can still pay, and the appendix reading lacks it. Extra stake, self-held or delegated, can raise the ceiling again; that cost is not computed. Honest high-fee nodes also need stake.

The emissions cap works on pool growth when it binds. Share-taking can survive in unused room or under the appendix weighting.

The claim delay works if the extra time supplies actionable evidence. The tested job is the one-round shape the paper argued for, but a sustained flow is untested. Honest income also waits.

The stake lock works if the opportunity cost outweighs the gain. Those costs were held at zero, so nothing is established. Principal is not lost.

Honesty scoring needs an authorized violation, established within acceptable error and effort, plus validators willing to act. No cell establishes it. A whole-round zero can reverse returns arithmetically, but no detection probability is supported.

The pattern: each defense has real reach and a condition under which it stops.

## Nineteen. Completions compared

The tests compared plausible ways to finish the paper.

Choosing the main-text reading keeps a stake defense. Choosing the appendix removes it. Choosing the first concept leaves pool-growth incentives below its cap. Fixed or shrinking pools remove that channel but can leave share-taking, and the second concept shrinks honest rewards as fees grow. Where withheld reward goes, whether held, burned or redistributed, changes supply and recipients and needs an explicit rule. Narrow self-dealing criteria leave the unmodified transparent case outside the allegation. Broad criteria need evidence beyond service records.

None of these choices defines the missing contribution. That is the common thread.

## Twenty. The conclusions

Four conclusions came out, each held at inference.

One. Conditional returns are established. Some added jobs gain and some lose, under stated controls. The arithmetic is supported. The significance is unresolved, because the paper does not say what the reward should buy.

Two. Assumed timely disclosure can clear the lookalike without establishing the paired violation.

Three. Defenses have distinct operating conditions and bounded reach.

Four. M3 receives contribution and evidence gaps alongside the paper's retained strengths.

## Twenty-one. The handoff to M3

Three items, each with its counterevidence.

The contribution gap. The paper leaves undefined what benefit is required beyond real, honest paid work. Positive returns and unknown subsidy value leave that open under every completion compared. If the paper's objective accepts this work as honest, the concern narrows, regardless of profit.

The conditional reward concern. If a defined contribution excludes these jobs, positive regions survive plausible completions even with working ceilings and costs. Deferred variants can move that either way. It is not a verdict of abuse.

The evidence and response gap. Where different treatment is required, neither tested standard establishes the violation. Explanation-based treatment exposes honest undisclosed testing. Safe access and contest costs are unaddressed.

And the strengths travel with them. Outside service earns a separate cash cut. The stake minimum and cap work in named regions. Costs defeat self-funding in others. The honest-work objective sets no independent-customer requirement. Scoring targets dishonesty, not service quality. Shared tooling and mutual accountability are intended supports. Timely testing disclosure protects legitimate work under assumptions.

A bounded follow-up is recommended, covering outside ownership, higher cost, later ratios and the lower baseline of twenty-eight point eight, which the paper's own first-quarter figures imply. It can move signs either way. Outside ownership and higher cost lower the return. A lower ratio lowers it for small additions, but it also delays the cap, so for larger additions the break-even can move out. At twenty percent stake, for example, it rises from about three times existing fees to nearly nine times at ratio eight. That is why the follow-up is not a one-direction check.

Specification and accounting rules remain build work.

## Twenty-two. What the slice does not claim

It does not claim that self-payment is profitable, or that it isn't. It does not claim anyone does it. It does not claim that validators can or cannot tell cases apart in general. It does not claim the paper is flawed. It chooses no emissions rule, no reward reading and no self-dealing standard. It does not model responses by other participants.

And it is one slice. Validator incentives, delegation, access, rule changes and the token price are untouched.

## Twenty-three. How much weight the results can bear

A reviewer should know what safeguards are in the work, and where it is thin.

The safeguards. The calculation can be rerun from recorded inputs, and a separate checker uses constants entered independently of its outputs. The build refuses to run if the litepaper snapshot has changed. The evidence tolerances were committed before any packet or verdict existed, so they could not be tuned to the outcome. Both readings of when to cut were carried, even where one fails the tolerance. Where disclosure clears the testing case, the result is stated each time as resting on an assumption.

Where it is thin. Everything is illustrative. Costs, ownership, prices and the median are controls, not measurements. The evidence packet is synthetic, written by the researchers, and its judgments were checked by hand. The evidence checker verifies structure and allowed values. It does not check the judgments themselves. So a reader who disagrees with a verdict is arguing with the reasoning, and the reasoning is where to look.

On status. Of fifty-two findings in the workbench, twelve are marked supported, twelve inference, twenty-two assumption and six hypothesis. The newer findings from this slice are mostly supported, because they are arithmetic under stated controls, or inference, because they rest on the synthetic packet. The records name no reviewer for any of them yet, and the experiments are marked drafted.

## Twenty-four. What would change the picture

Each conclusion carries its counterevidence.

The return result weakens in any region where costs or ownership make every added job lose. It strengthens only if the missing contribution is defined and the return is positive under named assumptions. Accepting these jobs as honest work narrows it either way.

The evidence result would narrow if an obtainable observation separated the pair, for example an affirmative public record of the alleged misrepresentation. It would strengthen if a pair shared every observation a validator could get by the first claim. A release plan that is late, unauthentic, poorly linked or too sensitive to disclose removes even the conditional protection for testing.

The defense result changes with the deferred variants: added stake, the cost of locking, later ratios, unequal prices, and whether a median can recover. The follow-up exists to test those.

And the whole picture changes if the paper settles the reward objective. If it says honest internal work counts, the concern narrows. If it says the reward is for demand beyond the operator's own, the positive regions become the concern. That is a policy choice, and it is not M2's to make.

## Twenty-five. For the reviewer

Is a slice the right unit to approve? All three steps remain in progress. The milestone overview and README still describe M2.1 as handoff received and nothing run, and M2.2 and M2.3 as not started. That is out of date.

Are the base readings fair? The two reward readings are the clearest case. Would a different pick have been equally natural?

Is the money result stated at the right strength? Mixed signs under stated controls, a supported arithmetic, an unresolved significance.

Do the two standards and two readings of when to cut span the real disagreement?

Is the evidence test fair, given it is synthetic and the disclosure is one-sided by construction?

Does the handoff to M3 describe gaps, not faults, and keep the retained strengths beside them?

Does any sentence slide from a conditional into a conclusion?

And the open practitioner questions, such as how fast stake really moves and what share of an API relay's price goes upstream, remain unanswered. They are where the slice knows it is guessing.

## Twenty-six. One paragraph

Slice one asked whether paying your own node for real jobs can beat its cost, and whether validators could tell. The answer is conditional. The paper's defenses work in named regions and fail in others, depending on rules the paper has not chosen. Evidence for the one pair tested does not separate the cases, and the readings of when to cut differ in who bears the risk. Underneath both sits an undefined question: what the reward is meant to buy. The slice hands M3 that gap, the conditional concern and the evidence gap, with the paper's strengths beside them, and claims no verdict.
