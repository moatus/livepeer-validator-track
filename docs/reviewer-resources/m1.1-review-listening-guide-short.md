# M1.1 Review: Short Listening Guide

About twelve minutes. Built only from the validator-track workbench and repository, as at commit eec258d. No identifiers are spoken. A short reference list sits at the end.

**Status.** M1.1 is reviewed.

---

## One. The question

M1.1 asked: what mechanisms does the Livepeer two-point-oh litepaper propose or require? What does each do, what does each depend on, and which of its operations can be reproduced from defined inputs?

It starts from the paper alone. It carries two concerns, both framed as questions under test, not verdicts.

The first: validators are also operators, and the paper asks them to find self-dealing, fabricated fees and incorrect results in other operators' work. The evidence they can get may not separate abuse from legitimate work.

The second: exact is not the same as sound. A fully specified, fee-linked reward could still make it profitable to pay your own node for real work.

One rule governs the whole step. Where the paper is silent, that is recorded as something to build, never as a fault. A litepaper is a vision, so unwritten detail is expected.

---

## Two. The paper's story when everyone cooperates

A customer or agent picks a listed node by capability, region and price, and pays in USDC at a dollar price.

The fee splits. At the proposed starting values, half goes to the node. Half goes to the network, which buys LPT on the market and burns it.

Separately, a mint of new LPT pays rewards. These are two separate ledgers, dollars and tokens, never added together. The appendix proposes ninety-four percent of the mint for nodes, one percent for validators, five percent for the treasury.

A node's share of the node pool is the smaller of its fee share and its stake share. That is the Min-Fee-Stake rule. Fees can earn reward, but only up to what stake allows.

Rewards wait seven rounds before they can be claimed. At claim, the median of validators' scores, from zero to one, scales the reward. The delay is meant to give validators time to act on dishonesty.

Operators share with delegators through the cuts they set. Delegators are expected to move toward busy, under-staked nodes, which raises those nodes' ceilings. Leaving a node takes a proposed twenty-one rounds. Validators are the top thirty-three nodes by stake that opt in.

Two chains carry the design. Useful paid work earns fee-linked reward, which draws delegation, which supports better service. And evidence of dishonesty leads validators to act, which lowers rewards, which reduces the reason for dishonesty.

M1.1 recorded each arrow as an expectation, with the condition it needs. None is tested.

## Three. The twelve mechanisms

They fall into four groups.

**Finding paid work.** Registration lets any address become an operator and advertise, with no bond and no node cap. Open: what an advert contains, and whether anything checks it. Service discovery is how work finds a node. The paper gives no protocol rule for the choice. Open: does a brand-new listing get any work?

**Moving money.** Payment settlement splits each fee between node and network. Open: which rule ties a payment to a node and a period. Buyback and burn turns the network share into burned LPT. The method is left for later research. Open: at what price and time, and whether matching mint and burn in dollars means matching in tokens. Emissions determination sets how much to mint. The paper offers three draft concepts and has chosen none. Operator reward allocation is the center: it applies the smaller of fee share and stake share. Open: the paper describes the node reward in two different ways, and does not say where unassigned reward goes.

**Judging honesty.** Evidence collection gathers the testing and monitoring validators rely on. The paper suggests shared tooling funded as a public good, and names no payer. Honesty scoring has each validator score each node, and the median becomes the node's score. The score targets self-dealing, fee fabrication and incorrect results. It is not a quality grade. Open, and the hardest: which evidence warrants which score. Validator selection seats the top nodes by stake. Open: does stake rank say anything about independence, given that the largest operators are also competitors? Reward claiming applies the claim-time median. Open: seven rounds buy time, not evidence, and the paper doesn't say when a reward is final.

**Stake and rules.** Delegation lets delegators back operators. Open: can they see enough, and move soon enough? Governance can change parameters and replace the scoring implementation. Open: where does a policy change end and a decision about one node begin?

Note: the mechanism pages today also contain M1.2 work, the lens tables and critical questions. For M1.1, focus on purpose, operation, parts and design questions.

## Four. What can be computed, and what cannot

Anyone can reproduce the fee split, fee and stake shares, the smaller of the two, the division of each round's mint, the median, and the delay and unbonding counters.

Nobody can reproduce how a validator reaches a score: what evidence counts, what defines each kind of dishonesty, how a finding becomes a number. The paper itself calls reward eligibility inherently subjective. The workbench notes that this describes the judgment inside each score, not the median, which is mechanical.

Even the computable pile has one gap. With two descriptions of the node reward, there is no single payout until a reading is chosen.

The thirty-two open design questions are sorted by what would settle them. Nineteen involve a formula, which can simply be written. Eighteen involve outside data. Ten involve a judgment. Twenty-five involve a policy choice, someone with authority has to decide. A question can be several of these.

An illustration, with illustrative numbers. Fees split ninety and ten, stake split ten and ninety. Each node's smaller share is ten percent. Of a nine hundred forty token pool, seven hundred fifty-two has no stated destination.

## Five. The conclusions

Thirteen live conclusions, one superseded. In themes:

**Entry.** Anyone can sell. No bond, no node cap, no fee cap. Stake limits only the inflation reward share and validator seats. Whether newcomers actually get customers is unmeasured. Held as supported by the text.

**Unfixed rules.** The paper doesn't fix one node reward. And no emissions rule is chosen. Four links the design relies on are recorded as assumptions: settled fees track real work, delegators can move in time, buybacks execute well and balance the mint, and scoring rules make judgments credible. None is found failing. They are named so the handoff carries them.

**Validators.** Validators are asked to judge three kinds of dishonesty, but criteria, proof standard, quorum and constitution are open. The paper specifies how scores combine, not how each is produced. Fee fabrication, incorrect results and self-dealing each raise different evidence questions. A payment's recorded sender and its ultimate funder are different things, and common ownership alone is not misconduct.

**The central risk.** If fees drive reward, a party might pay its own node for real, correct work and collect more reward than the job cost. Proof of execution confirms the work, not independent demand or a worthwhile subsidy. The paper's responses, a stake cap, claim-time scoring, the seven-round delay and a mint cap in one draft, each limit part of the path. None is tested. This is held as a hypothesis. The workbench does not say it is profitable, and does not say it isn't.

A related conclusion replaced an earlier, stronger one. The earlier version said the reward never checks demand or value. It was withdrawn as an overstatement. The surviving claim is narrower: the stated arithmetic has no separate demand or subsidy-value term.

**Computable is not the same as sound.** Reward arithmetic is reproducible once open choices are fixed. But a written rule settles how a result is reached, not whether it rewards the intended behavior. Choices like the fee split and claim rules still shape incentives, even though they are exact. How participants respond is left to M2.1.

**What it takes to finish.** Six provisional groups of specification work, analytical and not adopted components. And accounting inputs differ: protocol records, market prices and validator assessments each need their own check. Writing a number down does not establish every claim attached to it.

## Six. Kept separate

A job raises different claims: payment, execution, correctness, availability, independent demand, economic value of the subsidy. The reward formula uses payment, stake and fee shares directly, and the dishonesty finding through the median. It has no direct input for independent demand or subsidy value.

That gap is where the central risk lives. A self-funded job can pass payment, execution and correctness. The open question is whether it reflects independent demand or deserves subsidy.

---

## Seven. Findings and one concrete result

Forty-five findings were recorded. Twenty-six are assumptions, mostly the paper's own stated rationales with the conditions each needs. Eleven are inferences, seven hypotheses, one supported.

The supported one is a check of the paper's own numbers. The first emissions concept starts at thirty-two times network fees, but the paper's own first-quarter figures give twenty-eight point eight. That is about eleven percent lower, and every later year derives from it. The paper cites no source. The conclusion is a request for a sourced figure, not a charge against the paper.

---

## Eight. What M1.1 does not claim

That the paper is flawed. That self-payment is profitable. That validators cannot assess honesty. That judgment-based means broken. That common ownership is misconduct. That any parameter is approved. And it prescribes no fix and introduces no new mechanism.

---

## Nine. For the reviewer

Does the split into twelve mechanisms feel right? Would any be merged or divided?

Do the two node-reward descriptions really conflict? This is the most concrete checkable item.

Does the paper settle any question listed as open, or leave open anything listed as specified?

Is each conclusion held at the right strength? Four are supported by text, eight are inference, one is a hypothesis.

Does any sentence slide from a gap into a fault?

Is the central risk stated neutrally, or does its prominence reflect the project's starting concern more than the paper's text?

Nine practitioner questions remain open, such as how fast stake really moves and what a real fee spike looks like. These are where the step knows it is guessing.

---

## Ten. One paragraph

M1.1 turned the litepaper into twelve named mechanisms and showed what the paper settles and what remains to build. It traced the cooperative flow and recorded each expected cause and effect as an expectation with a condition. It found that most reward arithmetic is reproducible once a few choices are made, that how a score is produced is the largest open piece, and that the node reward is described two ways. It kept a firm line between a rule being exact and a rule working, named self-funded real usage as the central untested risk, and handed thirteen conclusions forward.
